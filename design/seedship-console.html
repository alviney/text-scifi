Seedship · §8 interface design

Seedship Console

Ten mobile panes for the ship-management game, in the CLI idiom §8 calls for. The device is live — tap facilities, feed lines and equipment to drill in. Three tabs, a feed ticker above every screen, and one question per level answered in a sentence before a number. State shown is year 147, mid‑Long‑Dark: the reactor is failing, rare compounds are running out, and no engineer is awake. Swap the skin above the device — the layout never changes, only the treatment.

One question per level

Ship status is the calibration point — it earns its density because it answers exactly one thing: what needs me? Every level below it now answers one question too, and answers it in a sentence before it shows a number.

ShipWhat needs me?
FacilityWhat’s wrong in here?
EquipmentWhat do I do about it?
DetailsShow me the numbers — opt-in, never on the way past

Equipment used to open with twelve figures. It now opens with “Failing. It hasn’t been serviced in six years”, one bar, and the two numbers that decide anything — 780 kW made against 890 kW needed. The wear rate, repair count, fuel, coolant and ageing history all moved one tap away.

Nothing was deleted. It was moved off the path — which is what lets a complex system feel simple to walk through.

Rules are sentences, not code

The notation in plan.md §5c is a specification, not an interface. Players never see limitUnder(500) or postTask("service", front).

They see a rule named Restock water that reads: “When water drops below 500, add a job to restock it.” They build it by picking each underlined phrase from a menu, and the sentence reassembles itself as they go — so you can check it means what you meant before saving.

The diagnostics got the same treatment. DEAD · last fired 47y became “Never runs”, and the explanation is a plain sentence: the gauge says 900, there are really about 300, so the rule is never told the truth. THRASH became “Fighting itself”. STALLED became “Waiting 5 years”.

Where numbers survived

A number stays on the path only when it is the decision. 780 kW against 890 kW needed stays, because that gap is the whole problem. Four working years of pilot left stays, because it is the countdown to losing drones forever. 84 rare compounds, 90 days stays.

Condition percentages, wear rates and repair counts do not — a bar and the words “getting worse” carry the same decision, and the exact figures are one tap away for anyone who wants them.

Jump to a screen

Four tabs, one per thing the game is about

Voyage is where the ship is going. Rooms is its body. Crew is the people. Rules is your own mind — you are the ship’s AI, and automation is literally you.

Rules earned a tab despite attaching to equipment, because §5 made facilities and systems rule-holders too. A rule can live at any level of the hierarchy, so “which of mine is broken” genuinely has no single address. Moving it out, along with Jobs, leaves Rooms clean: ten rooms and nothing else.

Jobs belong to the crew, and the board runs both ways

Jobs are work people do, and the board’s failure is always “nobody can do this” — a crew problem wearing a facility costume. It sits under Crew now.

Jobs go down to the crew. Requests come back up.

Same object, opposite direction, and it gives §3’s withdrawal and §10’s crew agency a home in v1 with no LLM needed. Okonkwo asks to go back in the freezer. Haddad won’t work with Novak. Adeyemi asks to train someone. You can say no to any of it — and saying no costs morale, which finally gives happiness the mechanical teeth §3 asked for and never got.

The best line on that screen is the quiet one: “Vasquez was right about the gauge, four years ago.” Crew notice things your automation missed, and ignoring them has a record.

Every room keeps its own stores

There is no ship-wide pool. Consumables live in the room that uses them, and nothing moves unless somebody carries it — which means a delivery job, which means it can be automated, which means it can jam.

It costs about 11% more jobs across the voyage and 0.07% of the crew’s time, so it spends the idle roster §3 identified rather than overloading it. It also gives the Cargo Bay and the Loading Crane something to do, and makes a vented room’s contents a real loss.

Mostly it buys chains. The Rules tab shows a three-deep one, jammed at the bottom: Life Support wants filters, Engineering makes filters, Engineering needs ore — and the ore is sitting in the Cargo Bay because nobody picked up the delivery. Three simple rules, all correct, and the smelter has been idle eleven days. The room reporting the problem is not the room that has it.

You inherit thirteen rules

The ship launches with standing rules the departure crew left behind — seven room-to-room deliveries, three production orders, a fire alarm, a reactor service job, and a water restock. Enough that it runs itself on day one, with the entire maintenance layer conspicuously absent.

Nothing in the set is badly written. It comes apart because the ship changes and the rules cannot.

They can only use tier-0 conditions, because there is no Logic Core or Scheduler aboard at launch — so every one is a single threshold with no guard and no sense of time. Thresholds sized for eight crew drift wrong as the roster shrinks. The grow beds launch already out of phase, so “replant when empty” holds a working stagger for forty years and then collapses the first time a planting is missed. And around year 100 a worn gauge quietly kills the water rule — which is the one on this device, still listening to a liar at year 147.

That is the tutorial: working examples to read on hour one, and four decades of slow, legible failure after that. It answers most of Open Question 4 without a tutorial mode existing.

The ship is the hero on Rooms

§4 says room connectivity is flavour. That was true when inventory was a single global pool — but per-room stores changed the brief. Things physically move between rooms now, and a schematic is the only place that layer can be seen at all.

The held delivery is the whole §7 trap in one marker: the reactor is failing, so there is no power for the crane, so the ore can’t move, so the smelter is idle, so there are no parts — to fix the reactor.

Position means location, so the two markers cannot work the same way. Something moving belongs on the spine, travelling between its real source and destination — the filters marker runs from Engineering to Life Support because that is where they are going. Something held is not in transit at all: it sits in a room, so it is badged on that room. The ore warning is on the Cargo Bay because that is where the ore is.

Note the Cargo Bay still reads green. Its equipment is fine — the held ore is a logistics problem, not a room-health one, and collapsing the two would make the status dot mean two different things.

So the cutaway does three jobs rather than decorating: where every room is, what state it’s in, and what is moving between them right now. The filter delivery slides along the spine; the ore delivery sits stuck against the drive end with a warning, which is why the smelter has been idle eleven days.

Two decks around a spine, bow to the left and drive to the right, so the shape reads as a ship rather than a grid. Every bay is a tap target — the same destinations as the list below it, reached spatially instead of alphabetically. Over a few hundred hours the player will know where things are without reading the labels, which is the real prize.

It mirrors Voyage deliberately: hero above, detail below. Voyage answers where are we going; Rooms answers what shape are we in.

Both heroes show motion the numbers can’t — a starfield for a ship that cannot appear to move, and delivery markers for cargo that has no other representation.

Vital signs: the one place density belongs

Dense telemetry was rejected as a navigation model, and it should stay rejected. But there is a question no other screen in the game can answer: is the ship getting better or worse, and how fast?

Everywhere else shows the ship now. Power at 780 kW is a fact. Power falling from 1,000 to 780 across sixty years is the story — and only a sparkline carries it.

Five closed loops, the ones that kill everyone if they stop: power, oxygen, water, food, heat. Each gets a current value, twenty years of shape, a direction, and one plain sentence saying why — density plus prose, never instead of it.

Two of them pay off immediately. The food line is visibly jagged, swinging on a 36-day period, and that waveform is the grow-bed phase collapse from §5c — the trap diagnosed as a picture rather than a paragraph. “The average is fine. The average is not what people eat.” And the water line still falls correctly even though its number is a lie, because a worn gauge drifts its readings but not its shape.

It is opt-in, off the path, and one tap from Rooms — the same rung of the ladder as equipment Details. The closing line is the point of the whole screen: four of five are worse than twenty years ago, and none of them is an emergency today.

Every signal is marked the same way

[CRT][RCT][POWR]  The reactor is failing.
[WRN][LFS][RULE]  Water restock hasn’t run in 47 years.
[WRN][CRG][STOR]  Rare compounds run out in ~90 days.
[INF][HYD][MOVE]  Fertiliser delivered from Engineering.
[···][QTR][CREW]  Okonkwo: “third week of the same…”

The marker is metadata. The message is prose.

The player never decodes the prefix to understand a line — the sentence always says it in English. The codes exist so a feed holding tens of thousands of entries across three centuries can be scanned, filtered and recognised by pattern, which prose alone cannot do. Fixed width means the eye lands in the same place every time.

Level is severity, and doubles as the speed slider’s filter floor: CRT holds the ticker and trips snap-back, WRN always shows, INF is suppressed at high speed, ··· chatter only survives at 1×. Facility is one of ten rooms, plus SHP for ship-wide and CRW for a person rather than a place. Code is one of twelve kinds — POWR WEAR FAIL STOR MOVE MAKE JOBS RULE CREW NAVG FUEL ATMO — and they double as the filter chips on the full feed.

The two ends of the game end up in the same shape: [···][QTR][CREW] and [CRT][RCT][POWR].

The ticker is the game’s pulse

One feed item, at the top of every screen, always. New items replace the old one, so you can monitor the ship from anywhere without going and looking.

A serious alert turns the strip red and holds it there — the ticker stops rotating until you acknowledge it.

That hold is the visible half of §2’s snap-back. The same event that pins the strip drops the speed, and the same acknowledgement releases both. §5 already treats “unacknowledged for X hours” as a rule, so acknowledgement was in the design already — it just never had a surface.

At 8,760× items arrive faster than anyone reads, so an item holds for a minimum dwell and a +3 badge counts what stacked up behind it. That is §11’s severity floor doing its work: at 1× the strip carries crew chatter and small events, at full speed only warnings survive. The strip’s content tells you what speed you are at without showing a number.

Voyage carries the story

A progress bar says where you are. This says what it cost to get there: “Behind you: 38 rocks taken, 6 missed, 7 people dead. Ahead: 153 years.”

Two scales, because 300 years and the next decade are different questions. The whole voyage puts the Long Dark in front of you as a long stretch of nothing, which is honest. The thirty-year lane is the one you act on — and the hollow diamond is an object nobody surveyed before launch, so its composition is unknown rather than merely uncertain.

Fuel sits here because it is a property of the journey, not of a room. It reads as reassurance at year 147 — 190 years of margin against 153 to go — which is the truth. Fuel is not this ship’s problem. Rare compounds are.

Status is the only colour

The interface is monochrome amber. The single exception is state — ● ok, ◐ warn, ○ crit — so anything wrong is the only chromatic thing on screen and reads before you have parsed a word.

Colour never carries meaning alone: the glyph differs too, so the panes survive both a monochrome CRT reading and a colour-blind one.

Degraded readings are marked, not hidden

Inventory shows Water 900? and the automation console explains why: the source sensor is at 22%, and the true figure is nearer 300.

§5 says worn sensors lie rather than fail. The interface has to make that discoverable without explaining it — a trailing ? on any value sourced from a degraded instrument, and nothing more. The player learns what it means once.

The console shows symptoms, not rules

With 61 rules, an alphabetical list is useless. The console opens on flagged — dead, thrashing, stalled — and pushes the 58 healthy rules below the fold.

The stalled one matters most: fired 34 · unclaimed 1,847 d. Nothing is broken, the rule is correct, and no engineer has been awake for five years. That is a §3 problem wearing a §5 costume, and the console is the only place both halves are visible at once.

Panes carry their own consequence

Facility view shows three beds ripe simultaneously with one botanist — the synchronised-ripening trap from §5c, visible as a fact rather than a warning. Crew shows 4 pilot role-years left beside two lost drones. Nav shows 4 of 6 drones against a break-even of 6.

None of these say “you are in trouble”. They put adjacent numbers next to each other and let the player do the arithmetic, which is the entire register of the game.

Still open

The speed slider and snap-back controls live in the status bar here as a readout only — where the control itself goes on a phone is unresolved. The cycle timeline that §5c calls for (six beds across 36 days, overlaps highlighted) is stubbed as a button, not designed.
