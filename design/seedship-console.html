One question per level

Ship status is the calibration point — it earns its density because it answers exactly one thing: what needs me? Every level below it now answers one question too, and answers it in a sentence before it shows a number.

ShipWhat needs me?
FacilityWhat’s wrong in here?
EquipmentWhat do I do about it?
DetailsShow me the numbers — opt-in, never on the way past

Equipment used to open with twelve figures. It now opens with “Failing. It hasn’t been serviced in six years”, one bar, and the two numbers that decide anything — 780 kW made against 890 kW needed. The wear rate, repair count, fuel, coolant and ageing history all moved one tap away.

Nothing was deleted. It was moved off the path — which is what lets a complex system feel simple to walk through.

Rules are sentences, not code

The notation in plan.md §5c is a specification, not an interface. Players never see limitUnder(500) or postTask("service", front).

They see a rule named Restock water that reads: “When water drops below 500, add a job to restock it.” They build it by picking each underlined phrase from a menu, and the sentence reassembles itself as they go — so you can check it means what you meant before saving.

The diagnostics got the same treatment. DEAD · last fired 47y became “Never runs”, and the explanation is a plain sentence: the gauge says 900, there are really about 300, so the rule is never told the truth. THRASH became “Fighting itself”. STALLED became “Waiting 5 years”.

The one piece of notation kept is the trailing ? on a reading from a worn gauge — because it is a mark, not a word, and the Stores pane explains it once in plain English.

Where numbers survived

A number stays on the path only when it is the decision. 780 kW against 890 kW needed stays, because that gap is the whole problem. Four working years of pilot left stays, because it is the countdown to losing drones forever. 84 rare compounds, 90 days stays.

Condition percentages, wear rates and repair counts do not — a bar and the words “getting worse” carry the same decision, and the exact figures are one tap away for anyone who wants them.

Jump to a screen

Five tabs, everything else drills

§8 listed ten screens flat. Ten is too many for a mobile root, so five are tabs — Ship, Crew, Auto, Mfg, Nav, one per system the player actually manages — and the other five are reached by drilling into the thing they describe. Task board and Inventory hang off Ship; the three detail panes hang off their parent.

That keeps the promise §8 makes about the feed: every line is a link to its source. Tapping the reactor alert lands on Reactor Core, not on a filtered log.

Status is the only colour

The interface is monochrome amber. The single exception is state — ● ok, ◐ warn, ○ crit — so anything wrong is the only chromatic thing on screen and reads before you have parsed a word.

Colour never carries meaning alone: the glyph differs too, so the panes survive both a monochrome CRT reading and a colour-blind one.

Degraded readings are marked, not hidden

Inventory shows Water 900? and the automation console explains why: the source sensor is at 22%, and the true figure is nearer 300.

§5 says worn sensors lie rather than fail. The interface has to make that discoverable without explaining it — a trailing ? on any value sourced from a degraded instrument, and nothing more. The player learns what it means once.

The console shows symptoms, not rules

With 61 rules, an alphabetical list is useless. The console opens on flagged — dead, thrashing, stalled — and pushes the 58 healthy rules below the fold.

The stalled one matters most: fired 34 · unclaimed 1,847 d. Nothing is broken, the rule is correct, and no engineer has been awake for five years. That is a §3 problem wearing a §5 costume, and the console is the only place both halves are visible at once.

Panes carry their own consequence

Facility view shows three beds ripe simultaneously with one botanist — the synchronised-ripening trap from §5c, visible as a fact rather than a warning. Crew shows 4 pilot role-years left beside two lost drones. Nav shows 4 of 6 drones against a break-even of 6.

None of these say “you are in trouble”. They put adjacent numbers next to each other and let the player do the arithmetic, which is the entire register of the game.

Still open

The speed slider and snap-back controls live in the status bar here as a readout only — where the control itself goes on a phone is unresolved. The cycle timeline that §5c calls for (six beds across 36 days, overlaps highlighted) is stubbed as a button, not designed.
