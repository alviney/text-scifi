B · what it buys

Sixteen assets visible at once against seven facilities, with condition, duty, draw and a 5-point trend on every line. The trend column is the real gain: direction, not just state — you can see the reactor falling and the battery charging without opening either.

On the equipment pane it shows a 60-year condition history with service marks beneath it. That’s the §4 ageing curve made visible, which no amount of row-based detail achieves.

B · what it costs

Touch targets. Those rows are 15px tall — readable, not tappable. It needs a cursor model or a second interaction layer, and it fights §8’s “tap a facility” navigation directly.

It also assumes a fluent player. Hour one, RCT.CORE ○ 38 RUN 780 is noise; hour fifty it’s the fastest thing on the ship.

C · what it buys

The strongest fiction of the three. You are an AI reading your own log, and detail unfolds where the event is rather than in a pane you navigated to. The reactor alert carries its own consequences and its own actions inline.

It also solves something the other two fudge: the stalled task and the sleeping engineer appear in the same breath, because a stream can put cause next to effect without a screen boundary between them.

C · what it costs

Terrible for comparison. “Which of my ten facilities is worst?” is a scroll-and-remember problem, and answering “how much water do I have” means issuing a query rather than glancing.

The command prompt is also a promise. Once there’s a >, players will type — and a tap-only game that looks typeable is a broken contract on a phone.

Where I land

A for structure, B for detail panes, C for the feed. They aren’t exclusive, and treating them as one choice is the mistake.

Rows stay the navigation model — they are tappable and legible cold. But equipment detail should adopt B’s density, because the condition history and trend columns show the ageing curve that the whole game turns on, and a list of key-value pairs simply cannot. And the feed should adopt C’s inline expansion, so tapping an alert unfolds it in place instead of throwing you to another screen and losing the thread.

The one thing I’d avoid outright is C’s prompt. Everything else in it survives as a feed behaviour.
